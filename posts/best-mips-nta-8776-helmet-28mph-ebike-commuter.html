Best MIPS NTA-8776 Helmet for 28 mph E-Bike Commuters

I rode for a year in a regular bike helmet at 28 mph before someone at a bike shop asked me one question I couldn’t answer: what is your helmet actually rated for?

By VoltRide Gear · Day 1 · ~10 min read · Updated November 25, 2026

It was a Tuesday morning, I was late, and my Class 3 e-bike was doing exactly what it was built to do: 28 mph down a straight stretch of road, cars on my left, a bus stop coming up on my right. Then a car pulled out of a driveway. Not fast. Not aggressively. Just… out. I braked, swerved, and missed it by about a bike length. My heart was doing something I don’t think hearts are supposed to do.

I got to work and did the maths for the first time. At 15 mph, a fall is bad. At 28 mph, you’re carrying roughly three and a half times the energy — speed squared. And the helmet on my head? A perfectly good road-bike helmet: light, airy, comfortable — and designed for the speeds a person can reach by pedalling alone. Not for a motor holding 28 mph while I sat upright like a sail.

That afternoon I asked a bike shop what helmet I should be wearing. The guy behind the counter asked what class my bike was, how fast I actually rode, and whether I’d ever heard of NTA-8776. I hadn’t. This guide is what I learned next — what that standard means, what MIPS does and doesn’t do, and how to check fit and airflow before you buy.

Why 28 mph changes the helmet question

On a regular bike, most of your speed comes from your legs — you might touch 20 mph on a descent, but you’re usually cruising in the teens. A Class 3 e-bike is different: the motor holds you at 28 mph for mile after mile, on flat roads, with no effort. Your average speed is higher, your top speed is higher, and your crash speed is higher too.

Most helmets in bike shops were designed around the old world. The US CPSC standard, which nearly every bike helmet sold in America meets, tests impacts at speeds that match regular cycling. It’s a real standard, and a CPSC helmet is far better than no helmet. But it was written before commutes involved a motor holding 28 mph. That’s why the Dutch wrote NTA-8776 — a standard for faster e-bikes, with harder impact tests and wider coverage.

So the question isn’t “is my helmet good?” It’s “is my helmet good at 28 mph?” Those are different questions, and the answer to the first one doesn’t automatically answer the second.

What NTA-8776 actually certifies — and what it doesn’t

NTA-8776 is a Dutch standard, written for speed pedelecs — e-bikes that assist up to 45 km/h, about 28 mph. Yes, it’s Dutch, and no, that doesn’t make it irrelevant if you ride in the US. Standards don’t care about borders; physics doesn’t either. A few helmet makers now sell NTA-8776-certified models in America, and they’re easy to spot because they say so on the box and usually on a sticker inside the helmet.

What does it test? Two things. First, impact energy — NTA-8776 helmets are tested at higher impact speeds than regular CPSC helmets, so the foam absorbs more energy. Second, coverage — more protection at the back and sides, around the temples, where road helmets get thin and vent-heavy. Side by side, the NTA helmet looks more substantial, less like a colander. There’s a reason for that.

What it doesn’t do: it doesn’t make you crash-proof — no helmet does. It doesn’t test every crash angle or surface. And a helmet without the sticker isn’t useless — a good CPSC helmet with MIPS, worn correctly, is still serious protection. What NTA-8776 gives you is a helmet whose testing matches the speeds you actually ride. For a 28 mph commuter, that’s a pretty good point.

When you shop, look for “NTA-8776” printed on the helmet or the box — not just “meets safety standards” or “e-bike ready.” That last phrase is marketing. The sticker is the thing.

MIPS, explained without the marketing fog

Every helmet page mentions MIPS, usually in big letters, usually without explaining it. MIPS stands for Multi-directional Impact Protection System: a thin low-friction liner between the shell and your head. In an angled impact — and most real crashes are angled — that liner lets the helmet rotate a few millimetres instead of grabbing and twisting your head with it.

Why does that matter? Rotational forces are a big part of what makes head injuries serious. A straight-on impact is bad enough. A glancing, twisting impact adds a rotational element that your brain really doesn’t enjoy. MIPS is designed to reduce that rotational motion. It’s not magic and it’s not a force field — it’s a liner that slips a little. But the idea is sound, it’s backed by testing, and it’s now available in a huge range of helmets at very reasonable prices.

Here’s what I got wrong at first: I thought MIPS replaced the need for a good standard. It doesn’t. The standard is about how much energy the foam absorbs; MIPS is about how the helmet moves in an angled hit. You want both. An NTA-8776 helmet with MIPS gives a 28 mph rider the harder impact testing and the rotational protection.

Some brands use their own rotational systems — different names, same idea, and those can be fine too. What matters is that there’s something in there designed for angled impacts, and that the helmet meets a real standard.

Fit and ventilation at 28 mph — the checks that matter

The first NTA-8776 helmet I tried on felt wrong — it sat higher than my old helmet, and I spent ten minutes convinced I’d picked the wrong size. The shop guy laughed, kindly, and showed me my mistake: I’d been wearing my old helmet too far back for years. Two fingers above the eyebrows, he said. Level, not tilted.

A helmet that doesn’t fit right won’t protect you properly, no matter what sticker is inside it. One that sits too high leaves your forehead exposed; one that wobbles will move in a crash; one that’s too tight gives you a headache by mile three, and then you’ll start “forgetting” to wear it.

Do these checks in the shop, or at home the day it arrives:

  • Measure your head. A soft tape around your forehead, just above the eyebrows and ears. Match it to the maker’s size chart — small, medium and large mean different things on different brands.
  • The two-finger rule. The front edge should sit about two fingers’ width above your eyebrows. Level all round, not tilted back like a cap.
  • The shake test. Buckled and snug, shake your head side to side and up and down. The helmet should move your skin a little, not slide around freely.
  • The strap check. Side straps should form a V just below each ear, and the chin strap should be snug enough for two fingers underneath — no more.
  • Glasses and ears. Try your glasses on with the helmet. Some helmets pinch the arms or push them into your temples. If you ride in winter, check there’s room for a thin skull cap underneath.

Then there’s ventilation, and at 28 mph it matters in a way it doesn’t at 12 mph. You’re moving fast, so air is coming at you hard — but you’re also working less, because the motor is doing the work, so you sweat less than a road cyclist. The problem is that NTA-8776 helmets have more coverage and often fewer vents than a road helmet, so on a hot day they can feel warmer than you expect. Some riders buy the safest helmet they can find, wear it twice in July, and then go back to their old airy one. That’s the worst outcome of all.

Look for big front vents that channel air through, rear exhaust vents, and internal channels in the foam. Try the helmet on a warm day, or walk around the shop in it for five minutes — if your head is already sweating in air conditioning, it won’t get better on a hill in August. And a visor? Great for rain and low sun, but at 28 mph it can catch the wind and whistle. I took mine off after a week; my neighbour kept his on and loves it.

The 5-minute shop test

Put the helmet on, level, two fingers above the eyebrows. Shake your head — it should grip, not slide. Walk around for five minutes, glasses on. Now imagine it’s August: if anything already feels hot, tight, or noisy, try the next model.

What to check before you buy

Let’s put it together. You’re standing in a shop, or staring at a product page, and you want to know if this helmet is the right one. Run through this list. If a helmet ticks all of these, it’s a serious contender.

Non-negotiable

NTA-8776 certification

Look for the actual “NTA-8776” marking on the helmet or the box. Harder impact testing, wider coverage. “E-bike ready” on its own means nothing.

Strongly recommended

MIPS or similar rotational protection

A slip liner for angled impacts. Most real crashes are angled, and at 28 mph the rotational forces are bigger. Other brands’ own systems count too.

Check in person

Fit — your size, your head

Measure, try it on, do the shake test. Size labels vary by brand. A helmet that fits is worth more than a fancier sticker that wobbles.

Don’t skip this

Ventilation + weight

Big front vents, rear exhaust, internal channels. NTA helmets run warmer than road helmets, so airflow decides whether you’ll wear it in summer.

A few more things. A rear light mount is genuinely useful at commuting hours. Check the weight, too: NTA helmets are usually a bit heavier, often 350–450 g, and a heavy helmet can give you a sore neck by Friday. Hold it in your hand, then hold the next one. And check the replacement policy — most makers say to replace a helmet after any significant impact, even if it looks fine, and generally every three to five years.

FAQ

Do I really need an NTA-8776 helmet for a Class 3 e-bike?

It’s the standard written for the speeds a Class 3 bike actually reaches, with harder impact tests and wider coverage. A good CPSC helmet with MIPS is still far better than no helmet, but if you regularly ride at 28 mph in traffic, an NTA-8776 helmet is the one whose testing matches your ride.

Is MIPS worth it, or is it just marketing?

MIPS is a real slip liner designed to reduce rotational forces in angled impacts, and most real crashes are angled. It doesn’t replace a good safety standard — you want both — but a helmet with MIPS is a genuine step up from the same helmet without it.

Can I use my regular road bike helmet on my e-bike?

Yes, if your e-bike tops out at 20 mph and you ride mostly on quiet paths. At a sustained 28 mph, you’re riding faster than a road helmet was tested for, with less coverage at the back and sides. That’s the gap NTA-8776 fills.

How do I know if my helmet fits properly?

Level on your head, front edge about two fingers above your eyebrows, straps forming a V under each ear, chin strap snug enough for two fingers underneath. Shake your head — the helmet should grip your head, not slide around.

When should I replace my e-bike helmet?

After any significant crash or hard drop, even if it looks fine — the foam can be damaged invisibly. Otherwise, the general guidance is every three to five years, sooner if the straps, pads or fit system are worn out.

The verdict

If you commute on a Class 3 e-bike at 28 mph, get an NTA-8776-certified helmet with MIPS, in your measured size, with ventilation you can live with in summer. It’s the combination that matches the speed you actually ride: harder impact testing, wider coverage, and protection for angled crashes.

Don’t buy on the sticker alone. Try it on, do the shake test, check the price and the return policy, and make sure it fits with your glasses. The best helmet is the one that fits, stays comfortable at speed, and is on your head every ride — not hanging on a hook because it was too hot or too tight.
